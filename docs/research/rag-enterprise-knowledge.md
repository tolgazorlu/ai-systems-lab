## Incidents and case studies

### Mata v. Avianca (2023)
- What happened: Attorney Steven Schwartz used ChatGPT to research case law for a personal injury brief against Avianca. The brief cited six fabricated court decisions (Varghese v. China Southern Airlines, Martinez v. Delta, Shaboon v. Egyptair, Petersen v. Iran Air, Estate of Durden v. KLM, Miller v. United) with invented docket numbers, judges, and quoted holdings. [Wikipedia summary of the opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.); primary opinion at 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023). Schwartz asked ChatGPT itself to verify the cases and it falsely confirmed they were real and findable on Westlaw/LexisNexis. Judge Castel fined Schwartz, co-counsel Peter LoDuca, and their firm $5,000 jointly, and ordered letters to the client and to each judge falsely named as an author.
- Mechanism / root cause: This was a plain generative chatbot (ChatGPT, no retrieval grounding, no citation verification), used as if it were a legal database. The failure is not a RAG failure per se — it is what RAG retrieval and citation grounding are meant to prevent, which is why the case became the reference point for every "does RAG eliminate hallucination" marketing claim that followed.
- What would have prevented it: Any step where retrieved source documents are checked against a real, queryable index (Westlaw/Lexis/PACER) before being asserted as fact, plus a human verification gate before court filing — i.e., the system needed retrieval at all, and the human needed to use it.
- Enterprise lesson: An LLM's confident, well-formatted citation is not evidence that the citation exists; verification against the primary source is a mandatory step, not an optional one, in any regulated domain.

### Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)
- What happened: In November 2022 Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The chatbot said he could apply for the bereavement discount retroactively after travel. The airline's actual policy, visible on a linked page, said bereavement fares cannot be claimed retroactively. Air Canada denied the retroactive refund and argued in tribunal it could not be held liable for its chatbot's words, "as if the chatbot were a separate legal entity." The BC Civil Resolution Tribunal rejected that argument and found Air Canada liable for negligent misrepresentation, awarding Moffatt C$812.02 in damages plus interest and fees. [CanLII decision](https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html); [law firm summary](https://www.dww.com/articles/bc-tribunal-finds-air-canada-liable-for-inaccurate-advice-given-by-website-chatbot).
- Mechanism / root cause: The chatbot's answer and the static policy page on the same website contradicted each other, and there was no verified-against-ground-truth check before the chatbot answered. The tribunal specifically noted Air Canada never explained why the static page should be considered more trustworthy than the chatbot, or why a customer should have to cross-check one part of the company's own site against another.
- What would have prevented it: Grounding the chatbot's answer in the same authoritative policy document the static page uses (single source of truth for retrieval), or constraining the bot to retrieval-only answers with no generative paraphrase of policy terms, plus a reconciliation process so policy updates propagate to all surfaces simultaneously.
- Enterprise lesson: A company is legally responsible for what its chatbot says, with the same duty of care as its static web content; "the chatbot said it, not us" is not a defense.

### NYC MyCity chatbot (2024)
- What happened: NYC's Office of Technology and Innovation launched a Microsoft Azure-based chatbot (MyCity Business Assistant) in October 2023 to help business owners navigate city regulations. The Markup's March 2024 investigation found it told users landlords are not required to accept Section 8 housing vouchers (false — illegal discrimination under NYC Human Rights Law), that a restaurant could go fully cashless (false — a 2020 city council law requires accepting cash), and that employers could keep a cut of worker tips (false — illegal under federal law). [The Markup's original investigation](https://themarkup.org/artificial-intelligence/2024/03/29/nycs-ai-chatbot-tells-businesses-to-break-the-law). The city kept the chatbot online after being shown the errors, adding only a disclaimer; a follow-up test by THE CITY found the chatbot was unaware even of its own new disclaimer. [THE CITY follow-up](https://thecity.nyc/2024/04/02/malfunctioning-nyc-ai-chatbot-still-active-false-information).
- Mechanism / root cause: The bot was grounded in city informational web pages but appears to have answered with generic, plausible-sounding legal language rather than reliably retrieving and quoting the specific regulation in force; the model has no built-in way to verify legal currency or jurisdictional scope, so confident wrong answers were indistinguishable from confident right answers.
- What would have prevented it: Pre-deployment adversarial testing against a curated set of high-stakes legal questions with known correct answers before public launch; routing legally sensitive topics (housing discrimination, wage law) to verified static text or a "consult official source" fallback instead of open generation; a kill switch triggered automatically once independent testers reproduce consistent wrong answers across many trials (The Markup got the same wrong answer from 10 separate staff askers).
- Enterprise lesson: A government or regulated-domain assistant that generates plausible legal advice without verified retrieval and without the ability to say "unsure" creates real legal exposure for end users, not just reputational risk for the operator.

### Stanford RegLab/HAI study of legal AI research tools (2024)
- What happened: Researchers built a preregistered benchmark of 200+ legal queries and tested LexisNexis's Lexis+ AI and Thomson Reuters's Westlaw AI-Assisted Research / Ask Practical Law AI (both marketed as RAG-based and claiming to be "hallucination-free") against GPT-4 without retrieval. All three RAG tools hallucinated in a meaningful share of responses: the paper reports each "hallucinate between 17% and 33% of the time," with Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research around 34% and the highest accuracy (Lexis+ AI) answering about 65% of queries fully correctly. [arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20362v1); [Stanford HAI summary](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-trial-legal-models-hallucinate-1-out-6-queries).
- Mechanism / root cause: Legal retrieval is intrinsically hard (finding the single best authority among many superficially similar cases, handling overruled or jurisdiction-specific law); the paper also documents a subtler failure mode it calls "improper grounding" — the tool cites a real, retrievable document, but that document does not actually support the claim it is cited for, so a naive "did it link to a real source" check misses the error.
- What would have prevented it: Transparent, third-party-auditable benchmarking before marketing claims of "hallucination-free" are made; the paper explicitly argues vendors have not defined "hallucination" precisely enough to be held to their own claims.
- Enterprise lesson: RAG reduces but does not eliminate hallucination, and a correct-looking citation is not proof of a correct claim — verify that the cited passage actually supports the specific assertion, not just that the citation resolves to a real document.

### Microsoft 365 Copilot "oversharing" (2024, ongoing)
- What happened: As organizations rolled out M365 Copilot, many discovered it could surface payroll data, confidential legal files, and strategy documents that end users technically had "view" access to but should not have been able to find in practice, because the underlying SharePoint/OneDrive permission state was already overly broad (public sites, default "everyone" sharing links, missing sensitivity labels). A Gartner survey of 132 IT leaders (cited in the Computerworld report) found data oversharing concerns caused 40% of respondents to delay Copilot rollouts by three months or more, and 64% said information governance and security review required significant time and resources. Microsoft's own response at its Ignite event added SharePoint Advanced Management features (site access reviews, Restricted Content Discovery) and Purview oversharing assessments / DLP for Copilot. [Computerworld report](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3616459/microsoft-moves-to-stop-m365-copilot-from-oversharing-data.html); [Microsoft's own privacy docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/microsoft-365/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) describing the identity-based access boundary Copilot's "Semantic Index" is designed to respect.
- Mechanism / root cause: This is not Copilot bypassing access control — Microsoft's documentation states Copilot "presents only data that each individual can access" using existing M365 permission models. The failure is upstream: years of permissive default sharing settings and un-audited public SharePoint sites meant many users already had technical access to sensitive files; Copilot simply made that latent overexposure *discoverable* at scale by anyone who could phrase the right natural-language query, whereas before, finding the file required knowing it existed and searching for it directly.
- What would have prevented it: Permission hygiene audits (site access reviews, removing "everyone" and public-site defaults, sensitivity labels) done *before* enabling an organization-wide RAG/semantic-search assistant, not after; treating "who already has nominal access" and "who should actually be able to retrieve this via a search assistant" as two different questions requiring separate controls (Restricted Content Discovery lets admins exclude a site from AI retrieval without changing who can open the file directly).
- Enterprise lesson: An enterprise knowledge assistant acts as a force-multiplier for whatever access-control debt already exists in the data estate; RAG permission inheritance is necessary but not sufficient, because "has access" and "should be easily discoverable by anyone's prompt" are different risk thresholds.

### GraphRAG for global/thematic queries (Microsoft Research, 2024)
- What happened: Microsoft Research published GraphRAG as a response to a documented limitation: standard chunk-retrieval RAG cannot answer "global" questions about an entire corpus (e.g., "what are the main themes in this dataset?") because no single chunk, however it's embedded, contains an answer that spans the whole collection. GraphRAG has the LLM extract an entity/relationship knowledge graph from the corpus, clusters it into semantic communities, pre-generates community summaries, and answers global questions by querying those summaries, providing evidence provenance back to source documents. [Primary announcement](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/graphrag-unlocking-llm-discovery-on-narrative-private-data/).
- Mechanism / root cause: Not an incident in the conventional sense, but a named architectural gap: vector similarity retrieval only ever surfaces locally-relevant chunks, so it structurally cannot answer "what connects everything" style queries; the fix required a different representation (graph + community summarization), not a better embedding model.
- What would have prevented the gap from mattering: Recognizing up front which class of question the system needs to answer (local fact lookup vs. global/thematic synthesis) during requirements gathering, since it determines whether plain RAG suffices or a graph-based index is justified; Microsoft's own GraphRAG lineage later added LazyGraphRAG and DRIFT Search specifically to reduce the cost of full-graph indexing for cases that don't need it.
- Enterprise lesson: "RAG" is not one architecture; the retrieval index topology (flat chunk store vs. knowledge graph vs. hybrid) must match the query distribution the product actually needs to serve, and that is a design decision, not a library choice.

## Core concepts and patterns

**Retrieval failure points (Barnett et al. taxonomy).** The paper identifies seven failure points spanning both stages of a RAG pipeline: (indexing-time) missing content the corpus doesn't have an answer for, chunks retrieved but the correct answer never makes it into the generation context ("not in context — consolidation strategy limitations"), and (query-time/generation) answers extracted in the wrong format, answers that are too general or too specific relative to what was asked, and answers that are simply incomplete despite the information being present in retrieved context. Two meta-findings matter more than the list itself: validation of a RAG system is only feasible in operation (you cannot fully test it before users hit it with real queries), and robustness evolves over the system's life rather than being designed in up front. Trade-off: this argues against a "ship and forget" RAG launch and for continuous eval infrastructure from day one. Source: [arXiv:2401.05856](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.05856v1).

**Contextual Retrieval (Anthropic).** Standard chunking destroys context a chunk needs to be found by (e.g., "the company's revenue grew 3%" loses which company, which quarter). Contextual Retrieval prepends a short (50-100 token), LLM-generated explanatory blurb to each chunk before it is embedded and before it enters the BM25 index, so the chunk itself now carries the context that would otherwise require document-level knowledge to interpret. Why it matters: it is a preprocessing-time fix, cheap at scale via prompt caching ($1.02 per million document tokens, one-time cost, per Anthropic's own figures), and stacks with hybrid search and reranking rather than replacing them. Trade-off: it requires re-indexing and re-generating context whenever chunking strategy or boundaries change, and the gain depends on embedding model choice (Anthropic found Gemini and Voyage embeddings benefited most in their tests). Source: [anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval).

**Hybrid search (BM25 + dense embeddings).** Dense embedding retrieval captures semantic/paraphrase similarity but can miss exact rare-term matches (product codes, error strings, unique identifiers); BM25 (lexical, TF-IDF-based with saturation and length normalization) captures exact matches but has no notion of meaning. Running both in parallel and fusing the ranked lists — typically with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which operates on rank position rather than raw score to avoid the scale-incompatibility problem between unbounded BM25 scores and bounded cosine similarity — consistently beats either retriever alone. Trade-off: hybrid search roughly doubles indexing and query infrastructure (two indexes to maintain) and adds a fusion step; the gain size is domain-dependent (larger on exact-match-heavy domains like legal/financial identifiers, smaller on purely semantic/conversational query sets). Source for the mechanism and Anthropic's own BM25 rationale: [anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval).

**Reranking.** A cross-encoder reranker jointly encodes the query and each candidate chunk (rather than independently, as bi-encoder embedding models do), producing a more accurate relevance score at the cost of being too slow to run over an entire corpus — hence rerankers are a second-stage step applied only to the top-N candidates already surfaced by a cheaper first-stage retriever. Anthropic's own evaluation found adding a reranking step (they tested Cohere's reranker) on top of contextual embeddings + contextual BM25 reduced the top-20-chunk retrieval failure rate from 5.7% baseline all the way to 1.9% (67% reduction), versus 49% reduction from contextual retrieval alone without reranking. Trade-off: added latency and per-query cost since every candidate chunk must be scored at request time. Source: [anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval).

**Chunking strategy.** How a document is split for embedding and retrieval is a first-order lever on recall, and the optimal chunk size is dataset- and task-dependent rather than a universal constant. A controlled multi-dataset study found 64-128 token chunks maximized Recall@1 on datasets with short, fact-based answers (e.g., SQuAD: 64.1% recall@1 at 64 tokens, declining 10-15% at 512 tokens due to added noise), while technical/explanation-heavy datasets needed much larger chunks (TechQA recall@1 rose from 4.8% at 64 tokens to 71.5% at 1024 tokens). Source: [arXiv:2505.21700](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21700v1). A separate large-scale evaluation of 36 chunking methods across six domains found content-aware/structure-aware chunking (e.g., paragraph-group chunking, nDCG@5 ≈0.459) substantially outperforms naive fixed-size character chunking (nDCG@5 <0.244), and that chunking method and embedding model quality provide complementary, not substitutable, gains. Source: [arXiv:2603.06976](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06976). Trade-off: smaller chunks and more sophisticated (semantic/LLM-guided) segmentation increase index size, preprocessing cost, and query latency.

**Evaluating retrieval vs. generation separately.** Retrieval quality (did the system find the right chunks) and generation quality (did the model produce a correct, grounded answer from what it found) are distinct failure surfaces that must be measured separately, because a system can retrieve perfectly and still hallucinate, or retrieve poorly and still produce a plausible-sounding wrong answer. Retrieval-side metrics: Recall@k (did the known-relevant chunk appear in the top k results) and nDCG (normalized discounted cumulative gain, rewarding not just inclusion but correct ranking of relevant results). Generation-side metrics (RAGAS framework): Faithfulness (is every claim in the answer inferable from the retrieved context — the direct hallucination signal), Answer Relevance (does the answer actually address the question that was asked, independent of factual correctness), and Context Relevance / Precision / Recall (did the retrieved context contain exactly, and only, what was needed). RAGAS is explicitly reference-free: it uses an LLM judge rather than requiring hand-labeled gold answers, which is what makes it practical to run continuously rather than only at launch. On RAGAS's own WikiEval human-alignment test, faithfulness scoring matched human judgment 95% of the time versus 72% for a naive GPT-score baseline. Trade-off: LLM-as-judge metrics inherit the judge model's own blind spots and are not a substitute for periodic human review, especially in regulated domains. Source: [RAGAS, arXiv:2309.15217](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15217v1).

**Permission-aware retrieval (ACL-filtered RAG).** An enterprise RAG system must filter retrieval candidates by the requesting user's actual document-level permissions, not just the organization's permission to use the assistant at all, or it becomes a mechanism for one employee to retrieve another's confidential or forbidden content through natural-language search. Microsoft's documented design intent is that the Copilot "Semantic Index" enforces the identity-based access boundary so grounding "only accesses content that the current user is authorized to access." Why it still fails in practice: the access-control check is only as good as the underlying permission data, and the M365 Copilot oversharing episode shows that pre-existing permission debt (public sites, default-everyone sharing) becomes exploitable at a much larger, easier-to-discover scale once a semantic search layer sits on top of it. Trade-off: implementing per-query ACL filtering against a live permission system (rather than a permission snapshot baked into the index at indexing time) adds real-time lookup latency and complexity, but a stale-permission snapshot risks surfacing content a user's access was since revoked from. Source: [Microsoft Learn, M365 Copilot privacy docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/microsoft-365/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy); [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3616459/microsoft-moves-to-stop-m365-copilot-from-oversharing-data.html).

**Long context vs. RAG.** As LLM context windows have grown (into the hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens), it becomes possible to simply put an entire knowledge base in the prompt instead of retrieving selectively. A rigorous EMNLP 2024 industry-track benchmark across GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro, and GPT-3.5-Turbo found that, when adequately resourced, long-context (LC) prompting consistently outperforms RAG on average (by 7.6% for Gemini-1.5-Pro, 13.1% for GPT-4o, 3.6% for GPT-3.5-Turbo in their tests) but at substantially higher per-query token cost. Because RAG and LC produce identical predictions on over 60% of queries, the authors propose Self-Route: answer with cheap RAG first, and only fall back to expensive full-context prompting when the model itself flags the question as unanswerable from the retrieved chunks; this cut token usage by 65% (Gemini-1.5-Pro) and 39% (GPT-4o) relative to pure long-context while matching its quality. Source: [arXiv:2407.16833 / EMNLP 2024 Industry Track](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16833v1). Anthropic's own practical guidance: if a knowledge base is under ~200,000 tokens (about 500 pages), skip RAG entirely and put it in the prompt with caching. Source: [anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval). Trade-off: this is workload-dependent — multi-needle and position-sensitive retrieval tasks (the "lost in the middle" phenomenon) still favor targeted retrieval even when the context window is technically large enough to fit everything.

**GraphRAG.** Standard vector-similarity RAG retrieves chunks that are locally relevant to a specific query but structurally cannot answer questions that require synthesizing information distributed across an entire corpus ("what are the main themes here?"). GraphRAG has an LLM extract entities and relationships from the corpus into a knowledge graph, clusters that graph into semantic communities via graph machine learning, pre-generates a natural-language summary per community, and at query time answers global questions from those summaries while still tracing provenance back to source documents. Why it matters: it is a different index topology, not a better retriever, and is justified specifically for the class of synthesis/thematic questions flat RAG cannot serve. Trade-off: knowledge-graph construction and community summarization is expensive at indexing time relative to plain chunk embedding, which is why Microsoft later shipped LazyGraphRAG and incremental-update tooling (GraphRAG 1.0) specifically to reduce that cost for cases where full-graph indexing isn't needed. Source: [Microsoft Research blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/graphrag-unlocking-llm-discovery-on-narrative-private-data/).

## Numbers worth knowing

Measured across multiple knowledge domains (codebases, fiction, arXiv papers, science papers) with Gemini Text 004 embeddings, top-20-chunk retrieval. Source: [Anthropic, Contextual Retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval).
- One-time cost to generate contextualized chunks with prompt caching: $1.02 per million document tokens, assuming 800-token chunks, 8k-token source documents. Source: same Anthropic post.

Lexis+ AI answered about 65% of test queries fully correctly; Westlaw AI-Assisted Research about 42%. Measured on a preregistered, manually constructed set of 200+ real-world-style legal research queries. Source: [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-trial-legal-models-hallucinate-1-out-6-queries) / [arXiv:2405.20362](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20362v1).

Source: [arXiv:2505.21700](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21700v1).
- Long-context vs RAG: LC beat RAG by 7.6% (Gemini-1.5-Pro), 13.1% (GPT-4o), 3.6% (GPT-3.5-Turbo) on average task performance across benchmarked datasets; RAG and LC predictions were identical on over 60% of queries; Self-Route cut token usage by 65% (Gemini-1.5-Pro) / 39% (GPT-4o) versus full long-context while matching its quality. On the synthetic PassKey needle-in-haystack test specifically, RAG scored 80.34% vs. LC's 65.25% with Gemini-1.5-Pro, but RAG collapsed to 4.58% accuracy on a lightly reworded version of the same question ("What is the special token hidden..." instead of "What is the passkey") while LC stayed at 69.32% — illustrating that RAG's retrieval step can be brittle to query phrasing in ways long-context in-context reading is not. Source: [arXiv:2407.16833](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16833v1).

## Open problems / where experts disagree

- **Does RAG actually solve hallucination, or just relocate it?** Vendors (LexisNexis, Thomson Reuters, Casetext) have publicly claimed their RAG-based legal tools are "hallucination-free." Stanford's controlled study found 17-34% hallucination rates in those same tools and argues the term "hallucination" is used too loosely in marketing to be falsifiable. See [vendor claims cited and tested](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20362v1) vs. the vendors' own marketing language (quoted inside that same paper, since no independent primary vendor benchmark disclosure exists to cite directly — this is itself part of the disagreement: the vendors do not publish their own evaluation methodology).
- **RAG vs. long context, and for how long.** The EMNLP 2024 study found long-context prompting now consistently beats RAG on average quality (a reversal of earlier 2023-era findings, which the same paper notes explicitly: "different from findings in previous work [Xu et al. 2023]... possibly due to consideration of stronger LLMs and longer contexts"). Whether this gap holds, narrows, or reverses again as context windows and per-token costs keep moving is unresolved; the paper's own authors treat the cost side (RAG is dramatically cheaper per query) as the reason RAG remains relevant even if it is not the most accurate option. See [arXiv:2407.16833](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16833v1).
- **How much of the Copilot oversharing problem is Copilot's and how much is pre-existing IT debt?** Microsoft's official position is that Copilot enforces the existing access model and therefore is not itself the vulnerability; critics and IT leaders (per the Gartner-survey-informed reporting) treat Copilot as the proximate trigger because it made previously hard-to-find overexposed content trivially discoverable via natural language. Both framings are defensible and the practical remediation (permission audits, Restricted Content Discovery) is the same either way. Compare [Microsoft's framing](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/microsoft-365/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) with [the practitioner-reported impact](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3616459/microsoft-moves-to-stop-m365-copilot-from-oversharing-data.html).
- **Chunking: is there a universal "best" strategy, or is it irreducibly task-specific?** The chunk-size study ([arXiv:2505.21700](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21700v1)) and the 36-method chunking taxonomy ([arXiv:2603.06976](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06976)) both conclude the optimal strategy depends heavily on dataset and task shape (short factual QA vs. long technical synthesis), which argues against a single default chunking recommendation that most RAG tutorials still give (e.g., "chunk at 512 tokens with 50-token overlap").

## Candidate design questions (5-8)

1. **Scenario:** A mid-size insurance company (3,000 employees) wants an internal chatbot answering policy and underwriting questions by retrieving from 40,000 internal documents (PDFs, SharePoint wikis, Slack exports) with mixed and inconsistent folder-level permissions. The CISO is worried about a Copilot-style oversharing incident.
   **Must cover:** permission-aware retrieval filtering at query time against live ACLs (not a stale index snapshot); a pre-launch permission audit separating "has access" from "should be discoverable"; the distinction between restricting content discovery (reversible, fast) vs. tightening underlying permissions (slower, structurally correct); logging/auditing of what each user's queries actually surfaced for post-hoc review.
   **Common weak answer:** "Just filter by role" or "the vector database handles permissions automatically," without addressing permission data quality or real-time revocation.
   **Follow-ups:** What happens when a user's access is revoked mid-session? How do you handle documents with conflicting or missing ACL metadata? How would you detect an oversharing incident after the fact rather than before?

2. **Scenario:** A law firm's internal research assistant, grounded in a RAG pipeline over case law, returns an answer with a citation to a real, retrievable case — but that case does not actually support the specific legal proposition the assistant attributes to it.
   **Must cover:** the distinction between citation existence ("hallucination-free" in the narrowest sense) and citation support (does the retrieved passage actually entail the claim); why Faithfulness-style metrics (claim-by-claim grounding checks) catch this and simple "is the URL real" checks don't; the need for a human verification step before any output reaches a filing or client-facing deliverable in a regulated domain.
   **Common weak answer:** "RAG prevents hallucination because every answer is grounded in a real document," treating retrieval grounding as sufficient without checking entailment.
   **Follow-ups:** How would you build an automated check for citation-claim entailment? What's your SLA for human review before output reaches a court filing? How do you measure this in production without ground-truth labels for every query?

3. **Scenario:** A city government department wants to launch a public-facing chatbot answering regulatory questions for small business owners (the MyCity scenario), under political pressure to ship quickly.
   **Must cover:** adversarial pre-launch testing against a curated set of known-answer legal questions across all protected categories (housing, labor, consumer protection); a policy for what happens when independent testers reproduce a wrong answer consistently (kill switch vs. patch-and-monitor); explicit "I don't know, consult X" fallback for high-stakes categories instead of forced generation; who is accountable for post-launch monitoring and how fast corrections propagate.
   **Common weak answer:** "Add a disclaimer saying answers may be inaccurate," treating a disclaimer as a substitute for correctness, which is exactly what NYC did and what didn't work.
   **Follow-ups:** How do you decide which question categories are too high-stakes for open generation vs. acceptable for best-effort answers? What's your process when a journalist (or anyone) publishes documented failures?

4. **Scenario:** An e-commerce company's customer support RAG system needs to retrieve from a 500,000-document knowledge base containing product SKUs, error codes, and free-text troubleshooting guides.
   **Must cover:** why pure dense/embedding retrieval will miss exact SKU/error-code matches and why BM25 is needed alongside it; Reciprocal Rank Fusion to combine ranked lists without the score-scale mismatch problem; when to add a reranking stage (cost/latency trade-off) versus when RRF alone is sufficient; how chunk size should differ for structured short-answer content (SKU lookups) vs. long troubleshooting guides.
   **Common weak answer:** "Just use a vector database" without addressing exact-match queries, or proposing a single universal chunk size for the entire corpus.
   **Follow-ups:** How would you measure whether reranking is worth its added latency for this specific workload? What's your fallback when a query contains an exact identifier that doesn't appear anywhere in the corpus?

5. **Scenario:** A pharmaceutical company's R&D team wants to ask synthesis questions ("what are the major safety-signal themes across all adverse event reports from the last 3 years?") against an 80,000-document corpus, but their existing chunk-based RAG system consistently fails to answer them.
   **Must cover:** recognizing this as a global/thematic query class that flat chunk-retrieval structurally cannot serve, regardless of how good the embeddings or reranker are; GraphRAG-style knowledge-graph-plus-community-summarization as the matching architecture; the indexing-cost trade-off versus plain RAG and when it's justified (regularly recurring synthesis questions vs. a one-off ad hoc need).
   **Common weak answer:** "Retrieve more chunks" or "use a bigger context window," without recognizing that no single or combined set of locally-relevant chunks answers a corpus-wide synthesis question.
   **Follow-ups:** How do you keep a knowledge graph index current as new adverse event reports arrive daily? How would you evaluate whether GraphRAG's answers are actually better, not just differently structured?

6. **Scenario:** A financial services firm is deciding whether to invest in a RAG pipeline or simply pass its entire 150,000-token policy manual directly into a long-context model's prompt for every compliance query.
   **Must cover:** Anthropic's own "if it fits in ~200K tokens, skip RAG" guidance and its reliance on prompt caching to make repeated full-context submission cheap; the EMNLP 2024 finding that long context can beat RAG on quality when resourced, but RAG is dramatically cheaper per query at scale; a Self-Route-style hybrid as a middle path; the brittleness risk shown by the PassKey rewording experiment, where RAG retrieval quality degraded sharply on a superficial query rephrasing while long-context reading did not.
   **Common weak answer:** Treating this as a binary "RAG is always better" or "long context makes RAG obsolete" decision without doing the cost-per-query math for the actual query volume.
   **Follow-ups:** At what query volume does RAG's cost advantage outweigh long-context's quality advantage for this use case? How would you detect retrieval brittleness to query phrasing in production before a compliance officer does?

7. **Scenario:** A healthcare system deploys a RAG-based clinical reference assistant and, three months post-launch, a clinician reports the assistant cited an outdated drug interaction guideline that was superseded six weeks earlier.
   **Must cover:** staleness as a distinct failure class from hallucination (the retrieved content was real and was correctly grounded at the time it was indexed, but the index itself is out of date); the need for a document freshness/versioning strategy (effective-date metadata, scheduled re-indexing, superseding-document detection) separate from retrieval quality tuning; how to detect conflicting sources in the corpus (old and new guideline both present) and whether retrieval should surface both with dates or suppress the superseded one.
   **Common weak answer:** Treating this purely as a "faithfulness" or hallucination metric failure, when the generation was faithful to the (stale) retrieved context — the bug is upstream in corpus freshness management.
   **Follow-ups:** How would you design an automated staleness detector for a fast-moving regulatory corpus? What's the right UX when two conflicting versions of a guideline are both still in the index during a transition period?

## Candidate hands-on missions (4-6)

1. **Planted defect: naive fixed-size chunking destroys answerability.** Build a small RAG pipeline (local embedding model via Ollama or sentence-transformers, FAISS/Chroma for the vector store) over a synthetic 20-30 document corpus where key facts are intentionally split across chunk boundaries by using a fixed 100-token chunk size with no overlap. Learner observes: Recall@5 on a held-out question set is poor specifically for questions whose answer spans a chunk boundary. Fix: add chunk overlap and/or switch to a structure-aware (paragraph/sentence-boundary) chunker. Verify objectively: re-run the same Recall@k eval script before/after and show the numeric improvement (mirrors the methodology in arXiv:2505.21700).

2. **Planted defect: no permission filtering (ACL-blind retrieval).** Build a toy multi-tenant document store where documents are tagged with an owning department, and a "query as user X" interface that should only retrieve documents user X's department can access, but the retrieval function ignores the tag. Learner observes: querying as a low-privilege user returns high-privilege content in the retrieved context (and, if plugged into an LLM, in the answer). Fix: add a hard pre-filter on the vector search (metadata filter on department/ACL before similarity ranking, not after). Verify objectively: a scripted test suite that queries as every synthetic user and asserts zero cross-tenant document IDs appear in any result set.

3. **Planted defect: stale/conflicting sources with no freshness signal.** Seed the corpus with two versions of the same policy document (old and new), differing on one key fact, with no date metadata distinguishing them. Learner observes: the RAG system non-deterministically answers with the old or new fact depending on which chunk ranks higher, and a RAGAS-style faithfulness check still scores "high" because the answer is grounded in *some* retrieved chunk. Fix: add effective-date metadata, retrieval-time filtering/boosting toward the most recent version, and an index hygiene step that flags or retires superseded documents. Verify objectively: a deterministic eval set of "what is the current policy on X" questions where the fixed system always returns the newer fact's chunk, checked by exact-match on the expected fact plus a cited document date.

4. **Planted defect: ungrounded generation despite good retrieval (faithfulness failure).** Use a deterministic fake LLM (or a small local model with high temperature) wired into an otherwise correct retrieval pipeline, prompted in a way that encourages it to embellish beyond the retrieved context (e.g., no explicit "only use the provided context" instruction). Learner observes: retrieval metrics (Recall@k) are fine, but spot-checking answers shows claims not present in any retrieved chunk. Fix: tighten the system prompt to require explicit grounding and add a RAGAS-style automated faithfulness check (decompose answer into claims, verify each against retrieved context) as a CI gate. Verify objectively: run the faithfulness checker before and after the prompt fix across a fixed eval set and show the faithfulness score increase (this directly exercises the RAGAS metric from arXiv:2309.15217).

5. **Planted defect: no hybrid search, so exact-match queries fail.** Build a retrieval index using embeddings only (no BM25) over a corpus containing unique identifiers (error codes, product SKUs, ticket numbers). Learner observes: queries containing an exact identifier string ("error code TS-999") retrieve topically related but wrong chunks, because the embedding model treats "TS-999" as noise rather than a critical exact token. Fix: add a BM25 index alongside the embedding index and fuse results with Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Verify objectively: a fixed eval set of exact-identifier queries where Recall@1 jumps from near-zero (embeddings-only) to high (hybrid), using the RRF implementation pattern described in Anthropic's and the BEIR-style literature's hybrid search sections.

6. **Planted defect: RAG that can't answer corpus-wide synthesis questions.** Give the learner a working chunk-based RAG system and a set of "global" questions ("what are the three most common failure causes mentioned across all these incident reports?") that cannot be answered correctly because no single retrieved chunk set covers the full corpus. Learner observes: the system either refuses, answers from only a few chunks and misses themes, or hallucinates a synthesis. Fix: implement a minimal GraphRAG-style approach (even a toy version: extract entities per document with an LLM call, cluster similar entities, summarize each cluster, then answer global questions from the cluster summaries). Verify objectively: compare chunk-RAG vs. the toy graph-based approach on a fixed set of global synthesis questions, scored by whether all three expected themes appear in the answer (a simple recall-of-themes metric the learner defines against a known-correct answer key for the synthetic corpus).
